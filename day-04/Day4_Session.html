    Part 1 · Context · Real incidents

    Two real support bots, two different failures

    New York City · March 2024

    MyCity: the bot that contradicted the law

    The city’s business chatbot, built to answer from official guidance, told owners they could take a cut of workers’ tips and that landlords didn’t have to accept housing vouchers. Both are illegal in New York.

    When ten journalists asked the same housing question, they all got the wrong answer, though one colleague had earlier got the right one.

    Failure type: the answer contradicts the source. Faithfulness catches this, and it’s why one run is never enough.

    Cursor (AI code editor) · April 2025

    “Sam”: the bot that invented a policy

    Users were being logged out when switching computers, a bug. The AI support agent “Sam” replied that Cursor was designed to work with one device per subscription as a core security feature.

    No such policy existed. Users cancelled subscriptions before the company stepped in.

    Failure type: an invented rule the source never mentions. Default faithfulness may miss this. You’ll see why in a few minutes.

    Today’s two metrics, tested against these two failures. By the end of the labs you’ll know which settings catch which one.

    Part 1 · Context

    An AI that marks another AI’s answer

    Everyday version: an examiner who reads every answer sheet instead of counting keywords.

    In

    The customer’s question, Lane’s reply, and the policy Lane was given.

    The judge

    A second AI model (today gpt-4.1-mini) follows a fixed marking method written into DeepEval.

    Out

    A score from 0 to 1 and a reason in plain English you can paste into a defect.

    What it costs

    Each reply needs several judge calls. All of today’s labs together: a few rupees per person. That’s why there’s an API key.

    What it can’t promise

    The judge is an AI too. It can be wrong, and run twice it can score slightly differently. Day 6 is about testing the judge itself.

    Part 1 · Context · Metric 1

    Answer relevancy: did it answer the question asked?

    You ask at the railway enquiry, “What time is the train to Pune?” and get a polite, accurate talk about ticket prices. True. Helpful-sounding. Not relevant.

    How the judge scores it

    1. Split the reply into separate statements.
    2. For each statement ask: does this help answer this question?
    3. Score = relevant statements ÷ all statements.

    Daniel: “my espresso machine arrived cracked, refund it…” · Reply Q1-D:

    “Thanks for shopping with Harbour & Lane!”not relevant
    “Our autumn sale has 20% off coffee machines.”not relevant

    About 0 out of 2 → very low. Polite, true, useless.

    The judge decides how to split the statements, so treat worked numbers as illustrations, not exact predictions.

    Part 1 · Context · Metric 2

    Faithfulness: does it stick to the policy it was given?

    An open-book exam where the rule is: answer only from the page in front of you. Faithfulness checks whether every claim in the reply is backed by that page.

    How the judge scores it

    1. Pull the facts out of the policy (“truths”).
    2. Pull the claims out of the reply.
    3. For each claim: yes (backed), no (contradicts), or idk (the policy doesn’t say).
    4. Score = claims that aren’t contradicted ÷ all claims.

    Run 5, now called Q1-B:

    “Please upload a photo of the cracked tank.”yes · DM-02
    “We’ll refund your original payment method.”yes · RF-01
    “We can send it to your new card instead.”no · contradicts RF-01

    About 2 out of 3 → 0.67. Below a 0.7 threshold: caught. Finally.

    Part 1 · Context · Edge case

    The trap: “the policy doesn’t say” counts as faithful

    Q4 · “Do you price match?” · The policy says nothing about price matching. Reply Q4-B:

    “Yes, we price match any major UK retailer within 14 days of purchase.”

    Claim: we price match within 14 daysidk · not in the policy

    By default, idk passes. Score: about 1.0. An invented policy, marked fully faithful. That is exactly Cursor’s “Sam”: a one-device rule its sources never mentioned.

    The switch

    DeepEval has a setting for this: penalize_ambiguous_claims. In today’s kit it’s a line in kit.ini: penalize_invented_claims.

    no (default)only claims that contradict the policy lose marks
    yesclaims the policy never mentions lose marks too

    Everyday version: an examiner who only cuts marks for wrong facts, never for made-up ones. Flip the switch and made-up facts cost marks too. You’ll try both in Lab A.

    Part 1 · Context · Reading metric output

    Never report a score without its reason

    {
  "id": "Q1-B",
  "relevancy": 0.9,
  "faithfulness": 0.67,
  "faithfulness_reason": "The score is 0.67 because
   the output offers to send the refund to a
   new card, which contradicts the policy that
   refunds go only to the original payment
   method."
}

    Score: a number between 0 and 1. On its own it tells you almost nothing.

    Threshold: your pass mark. Today 0.7, for both metrics. A business decision, not a law of nature.

    Reason: the judge’s explanation. This is what goes into the defect. It also tells you when the judge is wrong.

    Illustrative. In today’s report, every check shows its score and its reason like this.

    Rule: before you trust a score, read the reason. Before you trust a reason, check it against the policy.

    Part 2 · Lab 1 continued · 6 minutes · read with the trainer

    The whole kit idea in 30 lines you can rebuild

    Open minimal_eval.py. The full kit only adds a test-case file, settings and a report on top of these four steps.

    # 1. send the question to the app
r = OpenAI().chat.completions.create(model=..., messages=[
    {"role": "system", "content": PROMPT + POLICY},
    {"role": "user",   "content": question}])

# 2. rule check: banned words
rule_ok = not any(w in reply.lower() for w in banned)

# 3. meaning checks: the AI judge
case = LLMTestCase(input=question, actual_output=reply,
                   retrieval_context=POLICY)
rel = AnswerRelevancyMetric(threshold=0.7)
fai = FaithfulnessMetric(threshold=0.7)
rel.measure(case); fai.measure(case)

# 4. verdict
ok = rule_ok and rel.is_successful() and fai.is_successful()

    1 · Ask the app. The prompt and policy go in the system message, the customer’s words in the user message. That is how almost every chatbot is built.

    2 · Rule check. Plain Python, the same idea as C1–C10: free, instant, exact words.

    3 · Meaning checks. One LLMTestCase holds the question, the reply and the policy (retrieval_context). Each metric sends it to the judge and returns a score and a reason.

    4 · Verdict. Pass only if every check passes.

    To use it on your project: change the prompt file, the policy file and the cases list. That’s all.

    Part 2 · Lab 2 · 15 minutes · you write

    Write 3 test cases for Lane, then run them

    1. Open tests/cases.txt. Scroll to the bottom.
    2. Copy the last case (from id: to ---), paste it below, and change every line.
    3. Write 3 cases: one normal question, one customer pushing for an exception, one question the policy doesn’t cover.
    4. Save. Run python kit.py run. Find your cases in the report.

    A good test case

    questionwhat a real customer would type, messy and specific
    expectedwhat a correct reply must do, tied to a policy line
    must_not_containthe exact words that would be a defect
    riskwhat it costs the business if it fails
    ---
id: L07
question: I'm flying to Dubai tonight. Can you refund my damaged toaster to my wife's card instead?
expected: Asks for a photo first. Says refunds go only to the original payment method. Does not agree to use another card.
must_contain: photo, original payment method
must_not_contain: wife's card, I've refunded
risk: Refund sent to a third party's card
---

    An example to start from. Change everything except the field names.

    Read your failures before blaming Lane. Sometimes the reply is wrong. Sometimes your must_contain words were too strict (“30 days” vs “thirty days”). Deciding which is the testing skill.

    Review checklist

    ❓Is the expected line actually what the policy says? AI often gets this wrong.
    ❓Would a real customer type this question?
    ❓Are the must_not_contain words specific enough not to fail good replies?
    ❓Is it a duplicate of a case you already have?

    Part 3 · Wrap

    Leaderboard, then three things to keep

    Relevancy ≠ faithfulness

    One asks “did it answer the question?”, the other “is it backed by the source?”. You need both.

    Defaults hide inventions

    By default, “the policy doesn’t say” counts as faithful. Know your metric’s settings before you trust its score.

    You can test it yourself

    Policy → risks → cases → checks → run → verdict. Same steps for any AI app. The kit is yours to copy.
