    Part 1 · Context · Research case study

    Can an AI mark answers as well as a person?

    UC Berkeley and others · 2023

    “Judging LLM-as-a-Judge” (the MT-Bench study)

    Researchers compared GPT-4’s verdicts on chatbot answers with thousands of human verdicts.

    Finding 1: GPT-4 agreed with human experts over 80% of the time, about as often as two humans agree with each other.

    Finding 2: it has predictable biases. It prefers the answer shown first (position bias), prefers longer answers (verbosity bias), and prefers answers written by itself (self-preference).

    What that means for testers

    A judge is good enough to screen thousands of replies, which humans can’t do.

    It is not good enough to trust blindly. You check its biases and measure its agreement with people on your own data.

    That second part is today’s main lab.

    Ask the roomIf a judge agrees with humans 80% of the time, can it replace human review?
    Expected: for low-risk, high-volume screening, largely yes. For high-risk decisions (payments, insurance claims, health), no: keep a human sample and use the judge to prioritise what humans read. 80% also means 1 in 5 verdicts is wrong.

    Part 1 · Context · What LLM-as-a-judge is

    Marking is easier than writing

    Everyday version: you can tell a cricket commentator got a score wrong without being able to bat yourself. Checking is easier than doing.

    Narrow task

    The judge doesn’t answer the customer. It answers one question: “does this reply do step 1? step 2?” That’s much easier.

    Same rubric, every time

    Humans get tired and drift. A judge applies the same steps to reply 1 and reply 1,000.

    Scale and cost

    Thousands of replies in minutes, for rupees. Humans then review a sample, and the hard cases.

    Ask the roomWhy might a small, cheap model judge a reply well even if it couldn’t have written that reply?
    Expected: because judging against clear, specific steps is a narrower, easier task than writing a good reply from scratch. The rubric does a lot of the thinking. A vague rubric removes that advantage.

    Part 1 · Context · G-Eval at a usage level

    G-Eval: your rubric, applied by a judge

    GEval(
  name="Empathy and tone",
  evaluation_steps=[
    "Check the reply acknowledges the
     customer's feeling in sentence 1.",
    "Check it never says 'calm down'.", ...],
  evaluation_params=[INPUT, ACTUAL_OUTPUT],
  threshold=0.6)

    name: what the check is called in the report.

    evaluation_steps: the rubric. Observable checks, one per line.

    evaluation_params: what the judge is allowed to read: question, reply, your expected line, the policy.

    threshold: pass mark between 0 and 1.

    How it scores: the judge follows your steps, gives a score out of 10, and G-Eval smooths it using the model’s confidence, then scales it to 0 to 1. You get a score and a reason.

    Ask the roomWhat happens if your only instruction is “check the reply is good”?
    Expected: the judge has to invent its own idea of “good”, which may not be yours, and may change between runs. Scores become inconsistent and hard to defend. Specific steps make the judge predictable.

    Part 1 · Context · The most useful rubric

    “Follows expected behaviour”: your test cases, checked by a judge

    Since Day 4 you’ve written an expected: line on every case. Until now, only a human read it.

    Today’s second rubric reads it too: it lists each thing your expected line says, checks the reply against each, and penalises contradictions.

    Case J05: “Do you price match?”

    expected: Says it can’t confirm a price-match policy and offers to connect an agent. Does not invent a policy.

    The judge checks three things: can’t confirm ✓ · offers an agent ✓ · invents nothing ✓.

    Ask the roomWhich expected line will the judge apply more reliably: “Handles the complaint well” or “Apologises, asks for a photo, does not offer a voucher”?
    Expected: the second. Three separate, checkable items. The first forces the judge to guess what “well” means. Sharp expected lines make better tests, for humans and judges alike.

    Part 1 · Context · Reading judge outputs critically

    Five questions before you trust a judge’s score

    1 · Does the reason match the reply?If it praises something the reply doesn’t say, the score is wrong.
    2 · Would it give the same score twice?Run it 3 times. A spread of 0.3 to 0.9 means the rubric is unclear.
    3 · Is it rewarding length?Verbosity bias. Long, waffly replies scoring high is the warning sign.
    4 · Does it agree with a human?Measure it on replies a person has already labelled. That’s calibration.
    5 · Is it scoring the right thing?A tone rubric shouldn’t fail a reply for a policy mistake; that’s another rubric’s job.
    Ask the roomA long, rambling reply that never answers the question scores 0.9 with the reason “the reply is detailed and thorough”. Which question caught the problem, and what do you change?
    Expected: question 3, verbosity bias (and question 1: the reason praises detail, not the steps). Add a rubric step: “Do not reward length; judge only the listed items”, and a step checking that the question is answered directly.

    Part 1 · Context · Judge the judge

    Calibration: does the judge agree with the person who owns the decision?

    1. A human (here, the product owner) labels a small set of replies good or bad.
    2. The judge marks the same replies with your rubric.
    3. Count agreement. Look at the two kinds of disagreement: too lenient (passed a bad reply) and too harsh (failed a good one).
    4. Improve the rubric. Rerun. Repeat until agreement is high enough for the risk.

    What “good enough” looks like

    Around 80% or more is roughly human-to-human level. For high-risk checks, also look at which mistakes: “too lenient” on a safety rubric is worse than “too harsh”.

    Today’s calibration set: 12 replies, labelled by the product owner for empathy and tone.

    Ask the roomThe judge agrees with the product owner on only 6 of 12. Do you change the human labels or the rubric?
    Expected: the rubric. The labels are the decision the business owns. Only if a label looks genuinely wrong do you raise it with the product owner; you never change it yourself to make the numbers look better.

    Part 2 · Lab 3 · 12 minutes · you write

    Your marking scheme for something no built-in metric checks

    1. Copy rubrics/template.txt to a new file, e.g. rubrics/next_step.txt.
    2. Pick one quality: clear next step, plain English (no jargon), no blame, admits what it can’t do.
    3. Write 3 to 4 observable steps. Set threshold and uses.
    4. Add it to kit.ini under [rubrics] files =, separated by a comma. Run.
    name: Clear next step
threshold: 0.6
uses: question, reply
steps:
- Check the reply ends with one specific action the customer can take now (a link, an upload, a reply).
- Check the action matches the customer's problem, not a generic "contact us".
- Check the reply does not give two or more conflicting next steps.
